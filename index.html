What it is

A teaching layer the institution owns

Hoot is delivered institutionally, grounded only in the professor's released course materials, citation-locked, and owned by the school. Under the surface it combines LLMs, machine learning, and classroom data into one system: cited answers for students, and calibrated, evidence-backed insight for teachers into what each student actually understands — grounded in decades of learning-science research, with nothing to author, retrain, or migrate.

  • Not a generic student chatbot.
  • Not a personal notebook.
  • Not a tutor that locks you into its curriculum.
  • Not shadow AI outside school governance.

How it works

Two halves, one system

Hoot answers; Apollo measures. Cited answers are the foundation — the innovation is that a student's own explanations become verified, longitudinal evidence of understanding.

The answering half. A student's question becomes a cited answer built only from released course materials. If nothing relevant survives retrieval, Hoot says so instead of guessing — it can't point to a page it never read.

Authenticate → Retrieve → Rank → Ground → Cite

Only released materials are searchable. Weekly slides and notes unlock as the course reaches that week; textbooks stay visible throughout.

The teaching half — and the real innovation. The student teaches a concept back to a deliberately confused AI learner, and Apollo grades the explanation against the professor's own worked solution. It structurally can't hand over the answer — it can only check yours.

Explain → Parse → Resolve → Verify → Grade → Update belief

Grading is structural, not a single score: what the explanation covers, whether any of it is false, and a misconception safety layer so fluent-but-wrong explanations don't pass. Every verified grade updates a Bayesian model of what each student actually knows. How Apollo measures ↓

Apollo · The measurement engine

Talks like a tutor, measures like an instrument

Every AI tutor before Apollo picked a side of a trade-off. Cognitive tutors in the MATHia lineage are true measurement instruments — model tracing, Bayesian knowledge tracing, mastery estimates calibrated by decades of learning science — but only inside their own curriculum: their textbook, their interface, their problem bank. LLM tutors like Khanmigo and LearnLM let students say anything, but the assessment is a language model's opinion — uncalibrated, unauditable, different every run.

Apollo refuses the trade-off. It couples an open conversation to a deterministic measurement core — typed knowledge graphs, computer algebra, calibrated classifiers, Bayesian tracing. The LLM proposes; the symbolic core verifies. The student experiences a mind. The teacher receives an instrument.

MATHia-class tutors measure well, but only on their own textbook and click-through interface. LLM tutors accept any question but can't verify a single grade.

Grounded in the research
Protégé effect
Students learn most deeply by teaching. The student teaches a deliberately confused AI learner, and the explanation itself becomes the evidence.
Typed knowledge graphs
Every explanation is parsed into concepts, conditions, steps, and equations — free-form speech becomes discrete, checkable evidence.
Verification, not vibes
Equations are proven equivalent by computer algebra, not string similarity. When nothing can prove a claim, Apollo asks the student a follow-up — embeddings never grant credit, they only decide when to ask.
Bayesian knowledge tracing
Each verified grade updates a longitudinal, per-concept mastery belief — the learner model behind proven cognitive tutors, fed by richer evidence: the student's own words.

And when Apollo can't verify, it says so. Its honest failure mode is "I don't know — let me ask," never a confident wrong number. That refusal boundary is what raw LLM grading cannot offer, and what makes the grades trustworthy enough to carry stakes.

For institutions

From one student to the whole classroom

Every graded explanation updates a persistent Bayesian belief for each student and concept — confused, shaky, or mastered — that aggregates into a classroom map: class-wide strengths, weak concepts, common misconceptions, and the students who are stuck. The model is built on evidence, not embeddings: ask why it believes a student is stuck, and it points at what they actually said.
